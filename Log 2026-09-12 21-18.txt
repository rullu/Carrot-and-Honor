STATUS
The 2D / 2.5D world-map settlement experiment is concluded successfully enough to move on. The accepted prototype direction is a transparent 2D settlement miniature placed in world space with proper grounding, normal depth interaction, and restrained strategic exaggeration.

This is a prototype acceptance, not approval of the current test artwork as final production art. The current image, "The Victim", contains obvious generated-art defects such as incomplete/awkward wall continuity and is only being used to validate scale, composition, hierarchy, silhouette, and map readability.

ACCEPTED VISUAL DIRECTION
Settlements should read as compact strategic miniatures in the spirit of classic Knights of Honor 1 / Knights of Honor 2 world-map settlement presentation.

Core rule:
Settlement importance must come from footprint, hierarchy, silhouette, landmarks, and structured density — not from oversized ordinary buildings or from crushing every element into a dense blob.

The desired middle ground is:
- compact enough to read as one cohesive strategic object
- substantial enough to feel important and worth fighting over
- internally readable rather than visually crushed
- not realistically spread across a huge geographic footprint
- not a circular or emblem-like pile of buildings
- strong enough that even the lowest development stage retains meaningful visual presence

PROVISIONAL SETTLEMENT DESIGN RULES
These rules are now accepted as the working settlement-composition standard and should be carried into later documentation/design records:

1. House scale remains broadly believable and consistent between development stages. Larger or richer settlements gain presence through more buildings, stronger structures, larger footprint, and stronger landmarks rather than inflated house size.

2. Every settlement needs visible breathing room. Streets, alleys, courtyards, market space, small gardens, and gaps between building clusters must remain readable.

3. Use a clear hierarchy. A settlement should normally have one dominant seat-of-power element — manor, keep, castle, hall, fortified tower, or equivalent depending on culture and development — with secondary civic/religious landmarks beneath it.

4. Castle / seat of power should be spatially related to the town but not mashed into it. It may sit uphill, toward one edge, behind an inner wall, or overlook the settlement. It should not consume the whole composition.

5. The outer silhouette should be irregular and recognizable rather than a perfect circle or square. Organic asymmetry is desirable because it helps settlements remain identifiable at distance.

6. Development stages should change structure and hierarchy, not merely size. Even a low-development settlement must feel like a place worth fighting over rather than a few insignificant huts lost inside a province.

7. The core settlement asset remains separate from modular province sites. Farms, monasteries, mines, quarries, harbors, and other external sites should remain separate assets/systems rather than being permanently fused into the town image.

8. Fixed-camera art direction is acceptable. Settlement assets are authored for the game's approximately 40-degree oblique strategy-camera language; arbitrary camera rotation is not required.

9. Strong readability at close/default gameplay zoom is mandatory. Extreme far-zoom readability should not be solved by endlessly increasing physical settlement scale. If later needed, zoom-dependent presentation or LOD can be considered separately.

10. No clipping or accidental visual cutoff is acceptable. Assets need sufficient transparent padding, and the Sprite3D presentation must ground the visible bottom of the artwork correctly at terrain height.

DENSITY / COMPOSITION TARGET
The experiment established that both extremes fail:
- earlier castle-heavy image: too cramped / too much visual mass packed together
- later broad image: too spread out / read more like a realistic city layout than one strategic-map object

The accepted target is the middle ground: a compact irregular settlement with readable internal structure.

A useful art-direction guide is approximately 70–75% built/structured visual mass and 25–30% readable open space. This is not a mathematical runtime rule; it is a visual design check.

A practical target for a medium prototype composition is roughly 20–30 houses/workshops plus a dominant seat of power, one secondary civic/religious landmark, and a readable market/town-center structure. Exact counts may vary by region and development stage.

FINAL BORDER TARGET
The desired final direction is a two-sided political border band rather than a
single thin center line.

Design intent:
- the mathematically true province boundary remains the center seam
- each adjacent province contributes one separate colored half-band
- both half-bands meet exactly at the actual border
- each band extends slightly inward into its own province
- color is strongest/readable at the shared seam
- opacity/color softly weakens toward the province interior
- subtle hatching / brushed / lightly striped texture gives the bands a painted
  medieval-map character rather than a clean modern UI strip
- bands should be broader and clearly readable at normal and far gameplay zoom
- presentation must remain visible without overpowering the terrain
- colors should use a restrained medieval / heraldic palette:
  faded crimson, dusty blue, moss/sage green, muted ochre, weathered plum,
  iron grey-blue, warm brown-red, and similar desaturated earthy pigments
- no neon colors
- no glow
- no bright hover outline
- no persistent selection outline
- no separate coastline border

Long-term color architecture should allow each side of a shared border to receive
a realm/province color dynamically rather than hard-coding one universal border
color. This will allow the same rendering system to support the future political
realm map.
